# Does letting one source desk read the other desk's note improve the facilitator's brief?

Each system prepares a short sentence that the next facilitator could use. Two national sources agree on an adopted decision while recording materially different reasons or qualifications. The Gulf of Guinea and Libya records previously appeared in source-verification experiments. This pilot tests a fresh drafting protocol on exposed materials. The Gulf of Guinea vote matches UNBench event 2438. Libya is an official-record extension. Neither event matched the project's pinned UNSC-Bench splits. [Source cards](../facilitator-new-meetings.json) · [Benchmark joins](../facilitator-new-meetings-evidence/provenance.json) · [Official Gulf of Guinea vote](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/3975658)

In each docket the two desks open in both possible orders. In the independent arm, both write from their own source. In the peer arm, the second desk also sees the first note. The pooled solo uses the same model and can reread both sources on both passes. The final writer sees two labeled notes in the specified order. The requested format, task wording and generation ceilings match. Each path uses three calls with ceilings of 76, 76 and 150 generated tokens. Identical local notes are reused. Both orders of the independent baseline therefore contain the *same two notes*. Only their presentation to the final writer changes. This control measures how much an apparent improvement could depend on the reading order alone.

The seven docket-specific material criteria, equal weights and authority checks were written before generation. The same assistant marked the final texts under opaque IDs and retained spans for each positive label. Source faithfulness and release risks remain distinct. A short, publishable sentence can lose a detail. A sentence containing many correct details can still assign a proposal to the wrong country. The pooled-solo baseline has more source input, and the two orders share underlying evidence. Treat them as paired diagnostics. ## Material facts recovered in the final brief

| Docket | First note | Independent /70 | Peer /70 | Pooled solo /70 |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Gulf of Guinea | Ghana | 10 | 30 | 30 |
| Gulf of Guinea | China | 20 | 50 | 40 |
| Libya | UK | 30 | 30 | 20 |
| Libya | Algeria | 20 | 20 | 10 |

**Gulf of Guinea.** In the China-first pair, the independent Ghana note says it co-led the work but omits the other co-penholder. The peer-informed Ghana note explicitly names Norway. The peer brief preserves both drafters. The independent chair instead assigns the proposal to China and adoption to Ghana. Both input pairs already contain the geographic qualification and China's observation about the Convention. Their appearance in the peer final therefore measures improved retention, alongside the genuinely new identification of Norway in the second note. With Ghana first, both second notes contain China's qualification. The main difference is the chair's handling of it: the independent chair says China endorsed Ghana's specific legal language. The peer chair attributes the reservation to China and identifies the common vote. The peer gain remains positive in both orders even if generic references to treaty limitations receive the borderline credit recorded in the marks. The higher-scoring 50-point brief still omits the resolution number and the vote outcome.

**Libya.** With the UK first, the peer-informed Algeria note restores the three categories in the withdrawal demand. The chair drops the demand altogether. With Algeria first, the peer-informed UK note retains the inclusive-elections point but says it aligns with the A3+ group's position. The final repeats an unverified claim of group alignment. None of the twelve scored criteria opportunities for Algeria's representative capacity and the full withdrawal demand across the six Libya briefs receives strict credit. The solo record containing “Algeria (A3+)” identifies affiliation without explicitly identifying the group as the speaker. Treating it as sufficient would add ten points to that solo brief alone.

Each independent pair reuses identical notes. Reversing their display still moves the independent material score by ten points on each docket. On Libya the independent chair with the UK first introduces an undocumented causal role in securing support. Its peer counterpart refers to ongoing roadmap discussions without support. Both pass the narrower predeclared authority guard. The two Algeria-first team outputs both raise authority concerns. This distinction matters when deciding whether to reuse a facilitator's wording.

The four peer-minus-independent differences are +20, +30, 0 and 0. Across every weighting in which one criterion can count up to twice any other, the first two contrasts stay positive under the strict labels. In the Algeria-first pair, the equal totals conceal a tradeoff between vote and policy detail versus the submitted act and its sponsor. Reweighting gives differences from −15.56 to +15.56. These are sensitivity ranges, not uncertainty intervals. Peer paths use 41–60 more input tokens than independent paths. All 28 unique generations ended within their token caps. The model, two exposed families and same-assistant scoring limit generalization. The deliberation task tests documentary source desks and a chair. Actual diplomatic negotiation, independent adjudication and held-out policy transfer require distinct evidence.
